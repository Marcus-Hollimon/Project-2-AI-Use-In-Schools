Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) refers to systems designed to perform tasks that have typically required human intelligence, such as reasoning, pattern recognition, and content generation.

Generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) is one type of AI that creates content—like text, images, videos, audio, or software code—in response to a prompt or request. This guide shares information and resources to help the UDC community leverage the benefits of generative AI, recognize its limitations, and develop the skills to use, evaluate, and cite generative AI outputs.

Always check with your instructor, supervisor, or publisher before using generative AI in your work.

AI tools are rapidly advancing, and discussions about their legal and ethical implications are evolving just as quickly. We will periodically update this guide to ensure the information shared is accurate and relevant.

Definitions are adapted from Artificial Intelligence Terms: A to Z Glossary (Coursera, 2025) and Generative AI Basics (MIT Sloan, n.d.).

• Artificial intelligence (AI): Artificial intelligence (AI) refers to the ability of machines or computer systems to perform tasks that normally require human intelligence, such as communication and speech recognition, visual perception, decision-making, and natural language understanding.

• Generative artificial intelligence (GenAI): Generative AI is a subfield of AI that uses generative models to create content, including text, video, code and images. Generative AI systems are trained using large amounts of existing data and information, so they can find and learn patterns for generating new content.

• Algorithm: A set of instructions or rules to follow in order to complete a specific task. Algorithms can be particularly useful when working with big data or machine learning. Data analysts may use algorithms to organize or analyze data, while data scientists may use algorithms to make predictions or build models.

• Machine learning (ML): A subset of AI that involves creating algorithms and models that enable machines to learn from data, forecast trends and behaviors without human assistance, and improve their performance over time through experience.

• Training data: The information or examples given to an AI system to enable it to learn, find patterns, and create new content.

• Natural language processing (NLP): A type of AI that enables computers to understand spoken and written human language. It encompasses text and speech processing, facilitating features such as text and speech recognition on devices.

• Large language model (LLM): A type of machine learning (ML) model that has been trained on large amounts of text data so that it can understand language and generate human-like text. Large language models are the foundation technology upon which many generative AI tools, like ChatGPT and Microsoft Copilot, are built and specialized.

• Token: A basic unit of text that a large language model (LLM) uses to understand and generate language. A token may be an entire word or a part of a word.

Definitions are adapted from Artificial Intelligence Terms: A to Z Glossary (Coursera, 2025) and Generative AI Basics (MIT Sloan, n.d.).

• Prompt: A specific task, instruction, or question you give to an AI system to guide its responses or actions. Also called an input. 

• Prompt engineering: The process of designing and refining prompts to guide AI systems to generate your desired responses or behaviors.

• Output: The response or content generated by an AI system in response to a user’s prompt or input. Outputs may be text, images, audio, video, or code.

Check out the Effective Prompts page to learn more about prompts and prompt engineering.

Definitions are adapted from Artificial Intelligence Terms: A to Z Glossary (Coursera, 2025) and Generative AI Basics (MIT Sloan, n.d.).

• Hallucination: False or misleading information generated by AI systems and presented as factual. Hallucinations may take the form of inaccuracies, contradictions, or partially or fully fabricated citations.

• Bias: In the context of AI, bias refers to systematic patterns of unfairness or distortion in an AI system’s outputs, often resulting from the data it was trained on, the assumptions built into its design, or the way it is used. Bias can lead AI systems to favor, disadvantage, misrepresent, or stereotype certain individuals or groups.

• Transparency: In the context of AI, transparency refers to the extent to which information about how an AI system is built, trained, and operates is made understandable and accessible to users and stakeholders. This can include clarity about the data used, the model’s decision-making process (or its limitations), and when AI is being used to generate or influence content.

• Deepfake: An image or recording that has been convincingly altered and manipulated using AI to misrepresent someone as doing or saying something that was not actually done or said.

Definitions are adapted from Artificial Intelligence Terms: A to Z Glossary (Coursera, 2025) and Generative AI Basics (MIT Sloan, n.d.).

• AI literacy: The ability to understand, evaluate, and appropriately use AI systems and AI-generated content. This includes knowing what AI can and cannot do, recognizing how AI-generated content is produced, and practicing careful judgment when using AI tools in academic, professional, or everyday contexts.

• AI ethics: Refers to the issues that we must consider to ensure that AI technologies are developed and used responsibly. This means adopting and implementing systems that support a safe, secure, unbiased, and environmentally friendly approach to AI.

• Human-in-the-loop (HITL): A process in which humans actively participate in the operation, supervision, or decision-making of an AI or automated system.

• Copyright: Copyright refers to legal protections for original, human-made works. Complex legal issues emerge when AI systems are trained on copyrighted, humanmade material or generate outputs that may closely resemble protected, humanmade content.

• Academic integrity: Ethical standards for producing honest scholarly work, including appropriate use of AI tools, proper attribution, and not misrepresenting AI-generated content as one’s own.

Definitions are adapted from Artificial Intelligence Terms: A to Z Glossary (Coursera, 2025) and Generative AI Basics (MIT Sloan, n.d.).

• Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG): An AI method that improves generated responses by first retrieving relevant information from external sources (such as websites, documents, or databases) and then using that information to produce an answer.

• AI agent: An AI system that can autonomously carry out tasks by planning, making decisions, and using tools or data sources to achieve a goal with limited step-by-step human input.
