BCA Guidance: Guidelines for Conduct of Hearing Panels

All attendees at a Hearing Panel will maintain confidentiality regarding participants, materials, information, and discussions. All attendees must be treated with respect and are expected to extend the same respect to others. OCS staff may remove any person from the hearing who disrupts the process or fails to respect other attendees.

  • Note: Approved by the BCA on October 2024

Attendance is limited to panel members, Reporting Parties and Responding Students, OCS staff members, approved support people, and witnesses. Witnesses are only permitted to attend during their testimony.

Reporting Parties, Responding Students, and witnesses may bring a support person to the hearing, as permitted by the Charter. A Responding Student or Reporting Party may bring additional support people only if the Conduct Advisor approves. In conformance with the Bylaws, the Responding Student and the Reporting Party are expected to speak for themselves at hearings. A support person may only provide private advice to the person they accompany.

BCA Guidance: Pilot Program On Use of Hearing Examiners

This Guidance endorses a pilot program on the use of Hearing Examiners in OCS Hearing Panels, based on the BCA’s belief that proper use of Hearing Examiners can enhance the smooth operation of hearings. BCA believes that using Hearing Examiners can promote the search for truth, while reducing the problematic impact that parties’ varying public-speaking and advocacy skill sets might otherwise have on outcomes. Nothing in this Guidance forecloses a hearing from proceeding without the participation of a Hearing Examiner if both OCS determines that it cannot secure a qualified individual without unduly delaying a Hearing Panel and all parties consent to proceeding without a Hearing Examiner. 

  • Note: Approved by the BCA on March 10, 2026

OCS is to recruit qualified individuals to serve as Hearing Examiners as described below. 

  • a. The Hearing Examiners will be drawn from a pool of lawyers with relevant experience who are willing to serve pro bono for the Stanford community, and who will be trained in preparation for their service.
  • b. In all events, these individuals must agree to act as neutral aids and advisers to the Panel, which is the only body with decision-making authority.
  • c. Hearing Examiners must have no familial or personal relationship to any of the parties or witnesses in the matter. 

 In any hearing in which a Hearing Examiner is participating, the Hearing Examiner will conduct the initial cross-examination of all parties and witnesses. The Hearing Examiner is to function as an impartial actor, conducting cross-examinations in a thorough and respectful manner with the lone objective of ensuring that all testimony is subject to appropriate inquiry so as best to support the search for the truth. 

  • a. Parties are invited (but not required) to suggest to the Hearing Examiner areas of questioning and proposed questions to cover. The Hearing Examiner has the unreviewable discretion to decide whether to adopt those suggestions. All suggestions are to be submitted through OCS, by a deadline fixed by OCS.
  • b. In any event, following the Hearing Examiner’s cross-examination, parties are entitled to conduct cross-examinations of their own, provided they may not ask questions that are functionally repetitive of those the Hearing Examiner already asked, and provided they comply with principles of relevance, admissibility, and civility. The Panel will enforce these rules as described in Section 3. 

In any hearing in which a Hearing Examiner is participating, the Hearing Examiner will be responsible for assisting the Panel to identify and analyze issues regarding relevance and general admissibility of evidence, as well as to maintain civility during the hearing. 

  • a. When a party seeks a Panel ruling on an evidentiary issue, the Hearing Examiner will serve as a neutral resource to answer panelists’ questions about principles relating to the question. At no time should the Hearing Examiner instruct the Panel how to decide the question. That responsibility rests always with the Panel.
  • b. When the Hearing Examiner determines that there is an evidentiary issue worthy of the Panel’s attention, the Hearing Examiner shall recess the hearing momentarily to confer privately with the Panel (in a neutral manner as described above).
  • c. Once the Panel has decided a general issue, the Hearing Examiner may enforce that decision without pausing the proceeding to consult with the Panel each time a party is contravening the Panel’s ruling. However, any panelist is free to call for a recess so that the Panel can assess the question.
  • d. The Hearing Examiner is not permitted to be present when the Panel deliberates on the ultimate questions of responsibility and possible sanctions. 

In any hearing in which a Hearing Examiner is participating, the Hearing Examiner will be responsible for assisting the Panel to decide issues that arise regarding interpretations of the 2023 Charter and /or BCA Bylaws. The Hearing Examiner will not be the one deciding such issues or instructing the panelists how to decide them. That responsibility rests always with the Panel. Rather, the Hearing Examiner is to provide the panelists impartial objective assistance on various ways to approach the issue, enabling the panelists to achieve a full understanding of the available interpretive tools and the competing considerations as the Panel renders its decision. 

It is the intent of the BCA that during the Fall Quarter of the 2027-2028 academic year, OCS will submit to the BCA an evaluation of the pilot program. The BCA will use this evaluation, together with information the BCA itself gathers, to decide whether the Pilot Program should be made permanent, should be abandoned, or should be modified in specified ways. 

BCA Guidance: Reducing the Level of a Concern

Pursuant to the Charter, within five days of OCS receiving a concern, the OCS Director must determine whether the matter should move forward and, if so, the appropriate level of review. This is done at a very preliminary stage. The Charter recognizes that if the concern proceeds, the ensuing investigation led by the Conduct Investigator may reveal facts different from those that were available to the Director in making these initial determinations. 

 Accordingly, the Charter provides that, during the course of the investigation, the Conduct Investigator may request that the Director “assign a higher level of review for the concern.” Section IVB.3. And, in the opposite direction, if the actual evidence reveals that no charge is appropriate after all, the Charter instructs the Conduct Investigator not to pursue charges—regardless of any decision the Director made based on the preliminary information available during the initial five days. The Charter does not, however, explicitly address whether, based on facts that emerge during an investigation, the Conduct Investigator may request that the Director assign a lower level of review for the concern.

With this Guidance, the BCA confirms that, just as a concern can be dropped based on facts learned through the investigation, and just as the level of review can be increased based on facts learned through the investigation, the level of review can be decreased based on facts learned through the investigation. Therefore, the BCA now makes it clear that OCS is empowered to reduce the level of a violation whenever the OCS Director concludes that the level would not have been set that high had the Director been privy at that time to the information that has emerged in the time since the level was initially set.

BCA Guidance: The OCS Director's Initial Decisions

Section IV.A.1 of the Stanford Student Conduct Charter of 2023 provides that, within five days of receiving a concern, the Director of OCS or their designee (the “Director”) is to determine (a) whether the allegations in the concern justify initiating formal processes and, (b) if so, which level of review is appropriate. There has been some uncertainty about whether the Director must make those decisions based purely on the contents of the written Concern that was filed, even if the Director concludes that some additional information is needed to make the two necessary determinations.

With this Guidance, BCA confirms that, in deciding whether a concern should go forward and, if so, under what level of review, the Director is entitled to make reasonable inquiries to secure an adequate understanding of the basic allegations into the “nature of the concern.” The Charter defines “nature of the concern” to include “the apparent severity and pattern of the alleged activity and its impact on other community members.” The BCA cautions that the Director’s role in this regard is not to conduct a full investigation into the weight of evidence supporting or undermining the allegations of the concern.

BCA Guidance: Stanford Proctoring Guidance

The Board on Conduct Affairs (BCA) approved the adoption of the Stanford Proctoring Bylaw May 22nd, 2026, which is currently under consideration by the Faculty Senate, Undergraduate Senate (UGS), Graduate Student Council (GSC), and University President. In the interim, it is being shared as the following Stanford Proctoring Guidance. 

The following guidance provides a framework for fair and effective in-person proctoring that supports academic honesty and upholds the principles of the Stanford Honor Code.

Note: The Charge to the AIWG explicitly excluded the consideration of remote proctoring, whether by software or through a human proctor connecting via the internet. Accordingly, there is currently no recommended guidance for remote proctoring that would ensure exam integrity. Given concerns about its ability to uphold the principles of the Stanford Honor Code fairly, remote proctoring is strongly discouraged.

Suggestions on implementation of these guidelines are elaborated upon in the Exam Integrity Best Practices document

Proctors oversee the administration of the exam, document observations of student conduct, and serve as a resource for student questions. All proctors should complete the brief self-paced online Stanford Proctor Training before proctoring at Stanford.

While supervising the exam, proctors should be minimally obtrusive and make reasonable efforts to limit distractions for students. Proctors should have a clear means of identifying students, so that they can make diligent observations of potentially concerning behavior during the exam, taking care not to misinterpret normal behaviors that may introduce unintentional bias (e.g., stretching, fidgeting, clothing adjustments). Proctors will not prevent students from completing the exam; they may, however, remove any unpermitted aid. Proctors may deviate from these procedures when circumstances warrant, provided they act in good faith, with the goal of maintaining fairness, consistency, and minimal disruption to students.

Proctors should report any observations to the primary instructor, who should review potential evidence and submit all reasonable suspicions of potential Honor Code violations to the Office of Community Standards.

Students should expect that most in-person exams will be proctored. While the Honor Code does not require students to report violations, they are encouraged to share any concerns with proctors or their instructor during or after the exam.

If a department or program elects to proctor examinations for a course, that practice should be applied consistently across all offerings of the course, including all quarters, sections, instructors, and alternative examination arrangements (e.g., OAE, Athletics, etc.) during an academic year. To facilitate consistent exam administration, course instructors must communicate clear exam instructions to any other entities administering exams (e.g., Centralized Testing Center, Athletics, etc.).

Generative AI Policy Guidance for Academic Use

Informed by peer institution policies, Stanford’s Law School Generative AI Policy, and first-year Stanford students, as drafted in COLLEGE 102: Citizenship in the 21st Century Winning Policy Memo. 

  • Note: Guidance adopted on October 15, 2026

Generative AI is rapidly changing how students learn and engage with academics. Stanford recognizes the opportunities these tools offer while also recognizing important considerations for learning, academic integrity, privacy, and responsible use. 

This guidance is intended to expand upon and recommend updates to the Board on Conduct Affairs' (BCA) February 2023 Generative AI guidance. The following guidance provides greater clarity as the capabilities and availability of AI tools have evolved and is informed by feedback from Stanford students, faculty, instructors, and staff, as well as approaches taken by peer institutions. Upon formal adoption by the BCA, this guidance is intended to replace the February 2023 guidance. 

Stanford is a university that supports the exploration of new frontiers and tools, including Generative AI. The purpose of this guidance is not to prohibit the use of Generative AI, but to provide a framework for its responsible use in support of meaningful human learning and interaction. Stanford also provides the university community with sponsored AI tools and resources, to help students and instructors explore these technologies with greater clarity about appropriate use, data protections, and associated risks. 

Consistent with the purposes of education at Stanford, this guidance provides a general baseline, with expected variability across courses, disciplines, and schools. This guidance focuses on meaningful human learning and interaction, with appropriate and transparent use of AI that is guided by instructors and clear for students. Instructors may establish different and more specific expectations for their courses and assignments, and students are responsible for following those expectations. 

2.1 Default Course AI Policy

Absent course-specific or assignment-specific Artificial Intelligence (AI) policies provided in writing by the instructor, the default honor code policy on generative AI use in Stanford courses is as follows: 

a. Permitted Uses of Generative AI - Students may use generative AI tools to practice and prepare for coursework, to support learning, improve comprehension, and practice skills and concepts (e.g., self-quizzing, engaging in Socratic dialogue in which the AI asks questions to check student comprehension, and requesting additional practice problems on course content). 

As a practical guideline: If the use of a human tutor is permitted for a given task, then it is likely that the use of generative AI as described in the examples above is similarly permitted. 

Students may also use generative AI tools to perform surface-level editing that does not materially alter the substance, analysis, organization, or argument of the work (e.g., correcting spelling, grammar, punctuation, formatting, or typographical errors). 

b. Unpermitted Uses of Generative AI - Students may not use AI tools to create deliverables (e.g., assignments, projects, presentations, essays, reports), or to replace interacting with course materials themselves. Students may not use AI tools to act as a proxy for themselves or others (e.g., by having an agentic tool act on a student’s behalf in a course environment), including in the completion of course requirements. 

Students are expected to fully understand, be able to explain, and elaborate on their thinking process and work related to any course components or deliverables. Instructors may ask students to explain and elaborate on their course work and exam answers during class, sections, labs, and in other forums.

Under this default course policy, disclosure of AI use in the specific permitted cases above is not necessary. However, students must document and disclose any other uses of AI related to coursework. 

This concludes the default honor code policy on generative AI, which applies in the absence of course-specific or assignment-specific AI policies provided in writing by the instructor. Subsequent sections address student responsibilities that apply independent of course or assignment AI policies, and guidance for instructors.

2.2 Student Responsibilities

Students have the following responsibilities related to their use of generative AI in academic settings, regardless of the specific AI course policy in use. 

Students are expected to consult with instructors if they are unsure about the permitted or unpermitted use of generative AI in their course. Students are responsible for understanding each of their course and assignment AI policies. 

When generative AI use is permitted, it must comply with existing academic integrity standards and citation norms, including those related to plagiarism and the accuracy of submitted work. Citations to sources that do not exist or other irregularities may prompt a review as part of an academic integrity concern. Students bear final responsibility for the validity and appropriateness of any coursework completed with the assistance of generative AI tools. 

During group work, each student is individually responsible for ensuring that their own use of AI follows course policy. 

AI technology and capabilities are always evolving, and students are responsible for ensuring that their course deliverables have not been created with unpermitted use of AI. Students are encouraged to learn about the AI capabilities in any software that they choose to use for coursework so that they know how to disable, enable, or prompt AI functions in accordance with course and assignment-specific policies. 

Students must not upload, share, or otherwise disseminate other individuals’ personally identifiable information, private information, or other sensitive data into AI tools unless those tools have been approved for use with such data and the intended use complies with applicable university policies. 

Students who upload, share, or otherwise disseminate course materials, instructor-created content, or other non-public academic resources should only use university-approved or institutionally licensed AI tools unless expressly permitted by the instructor or required as part of an approved course activity. Course materials submitted to publicly available AI tools may retain or use materials to train those public tools. Uploading materials to these tools may inadvertently share Stanford-created and copyright-protected materials.

2.3 Instructor Guidance

2.3.1. Instructor communication to students

Instructors are expected to clearly communicate all AI policies and expectations to students in a timely manner, at a minimum in the course syllabus. 

Instructors may determine what constitutes permissible and impermissible AI use in their courses and assignments. In the absence of course-specific or assignment-specific Artificial Intelligence (AI) guidelines, students will abide by the default policy above. 

Instructors are responsible for reviewing and assigning final evaluations of student work. In the spirit of supporting a culture and environment conducive to academic integrity, instructors are expected to disclose their own use of AI tools in their courses, particularly when it involves giving feedback to students. 

AI technology and capabilities are always evolving. Instructors are encouraged to determine whether software they assign includes AI capabilities and to provide students with guidance on their use. Guidance may include how to disable, enable, or prompt AI functions in accordance with course- and assignment-specific policies. The Academic Technology Tools browser may be a helpful supplemental resource for identifying AI capabilities in commonly used academic technology tools. 

AI detection tools are not currently used at Stanford. Unlike plagiarism detection tools, AI detection tools are not currently used in evaluating student work. Instructors should consult the Academic Technology Tools browser for current information about supported academic technology tools and their capabilities. 

To ensure accountability, some courses utilize methods which may include asking students to explain their understanding and elaborate on their thinking process. In the interest of supporting students requiring accommodations, instructors should also note oral or other forms of course follow-ups on assignments in the syllabus. As other new assessment methods emerge, instructors should similarly note them in the syllabus so that students have adequate information to inform their study strategies and to request accommodations when needed. 

If instructors plan to load student work into AI tools, or plan to assign students to use AI tools in courses or assignments, they should ensure that the tools are among Stanford-approved academic technology tools or are approved for the planned type of use according to the Stanford GenAI Tool Evaluation Matrix. New tools should be vetted and approved for Stanford instructional use; instructors should consult with Learning Technologies and Spaces on whether their AI tools and software meet university standards.

Instructors are responsible for ensuring that they use AI tools and software in a manner consistent with the Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act (FERPA) , AGM 1.6.1, and Stanford Minimum Privacy Standards. Note that student information typical to teaching contexts may be considered high-risk data because it is protected by FERPA. Instructors must not disseminate or upload personally identifiable information of students, and other private, sensitive, or copyrighted information into AI tools that have not been vetted and approved for such uses.

Withdrawal Statement BCA Guidance

As noted in the Student Conduct Charter of 2023, § IV A 4, only Responding Students who are contesting an allegation may submit a statement requesting the withdrawal of a concern. The statement should be confined to explaining why the concern is unwarranted; it may not discuss extenuating circumstances. Submitting such a statement to OCS commits all parties to proceed with an investigation unless the Reporting Party withdraws the concern.

(Adopted Spring 2025)

BCA Guidance: Enrollment Holds Related to Sanctions Fulfillment

  1. When OCS determines that a student has failed to fulfill their sanctions obligation once the established deadline has passed, OCS shall place a hold on the student’s enrollment for courses. This hold shall be lifted immediately upon the student’s showing that they have fulfilled their sanctions.
  2. A student may petition OCS for a reasonable extension of time within which to fulfill their sanctions. OCS shall grant such requests whenever, in the determination of OCS, the student has established good cause for the extension or under other extraordinary circumstances. Good cause for these purposes means that events outside the student’s control rendered it impossible or unreasonably burdensome to complete the sanctions by the originally established deadline.
